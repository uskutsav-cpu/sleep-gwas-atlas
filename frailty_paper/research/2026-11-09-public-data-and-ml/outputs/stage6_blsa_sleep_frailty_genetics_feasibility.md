# BLSA: possible genetics, insomnia and frailty data, access unconfirmed

The Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA) is a potentially relevant aging cohort. A [published BLSA sleep-genetics study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11317550/) analyzed an older subset with a short-sleep polygenic score, epigenetic outcomes and covariates (367 complete cases). It also reports the five-question Women's Health Initiative Insomnia Rating Scale (WHIIRS) for its score-analysis sample. That is stronger evidence of sleep and genetics measured in the same research sample than simply observing two separate BLSA publications. However, 367 is the sample for that study's particular models and selection; it is not the available sample for our variants, FI, FFS or joint endpoint. The original score relates to short sleep duration, not our insomnia–frailty prototypes. The authors excluded non-White participants from their PRS analysis because too few had genetic data, and noted relatively low WHIIRS reliability in their samples.

The paper reports historical genotyping on an Illumina 550k platform and imputation using HapMap CEU release 22 on NCBI Build 36. It does not verify direct or imputed coverage and quality for our 33 candidate variants. The [2025 frailty proteomics study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12419846/) separately reports a BLSA FI analysis; its FI item inventory did not explicitly name insomnia or sleep, although it included effort and getting going, depression and physical function. As already documented in the [protein-panel review](stage6_proteomic_frailty_public_panel_review.md), 654 is that study's BLSA protein-FI analysis count, not a confirmed overlap with the 367-person sleep genetics study. A sleep-free label cannot be assumed for every BLSA FI definition.

The [National Institute on Aging](https://www.nia.nih.gov/research/data-sharing-resources-researchers) says researchers can apply to use BLSA data. The sleep-genetics publication states that data are available by request and committee review; another [BLSA publication](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11493922/) says data requests are subject to NIH IRB approval. No unrestricted individual-level download or total no-fee access for this exact project was verified. Published phenotype and genetic samples may overlap, but their intersection, consent, permissible linkage, marker quality, ancestry, sample/relative overlap with discovery and FI/FFS ascertainment remain unknown. BLSA is a secondary controlled-access lead, not a completed validation or confirmed free cohort.

If a permitted, affordable dataset becomes available, ask for a sample-flow count with variant-level genotype/imputation QC, WHIIRS and FI/FFS at specified visits, and access terms before analysis. Frailty and insomnia can be assessed separately and, when temporally appropriate, as a joint outcome. A small selected aging cohort is likely to yield imprecise performance estimates, especially for joint binary outcomes; the actual number of events is unknown.

## dbGaP accession check

The public metadata for [phs000215.v2.p1](https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/dbgap/studies/phs000215/phs000215.v2.p1/) describes an InCHIANTI/BLSA biomarker association release. Its [distribution manifest](https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/dbgap/studies/phs000215/phs000215.v2.p1/manifest/manifest_phs000215.Iron.v2.p1.c1.GRU.pdf) explicitly describes **summary-level data**, reports **zero individuals** in the distribution set, and lists association-analysis archives rather than individual genotype files. Therefore this particular accession is not a source from which to compute our participant-level scores. This finding does not rule out a separate BLSA genetic release or an approved direct BLSA request, and it does not establish that the study has no individual genotypes. A separately permitted genetic source and linkage to sleep/frailty would still need to be verified.
