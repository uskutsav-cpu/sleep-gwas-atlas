# COSFAMM: insomnia, Fried frailty and peripheral clock-gene expression

Checked 29 October 2026. [Ortiz-Vázquez et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1002/agm2.70117) report a cross-sectional analysis of the sixth wave of COSFAMM, an older-adult cohort in Mexico City. The authors report 751 interview participants; insomnia was assessed with the eight-item Athens Insomnia Scale (cutoff ≥6), chronotype with the Morningness–Eveningness Questionnaire, and frailty using the five Fried criteria. These are directly relevant phenotypes, although neither scale is identical to every discovery-study measure. The frailty phenotype includes exhaustion and the insomnia scale includes daytime consequences, so related content should be assessed before attributing their association to distinct biological mechanisms.

In a smaller peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) subset the paper reports clock-gene expression by RT-qPCR, not single-cell profiling or variant associations. The abstract and methods state **82 participants**; the description of Supplementary Table S3 lists 8 robust, 39 prefrail and 36 frail participants, which adds to **83**. The discrepancy is unresolved and must be checked against the underlying data before analysis. Morning blood samples were obtained between 8 and 10 a.m.; a single time window cannot resolve circadian amplitude, phase or 24-hour rhythmicity, and PBMC expression can reflect cell composition. The authors report lower CLOCK expression in prefrail versus robust participants after their stated adjustment and multiple-testing procedure. This is a small observational comparison, not proof that altered CLOCK expression causes frailty, that our score variants regulate CLOCK, or that insomnia mediates the relationship. The authors also report a lower odds of insomnia for extreme-morning compared with intermediate chronotype (adjusted OR 0.393, 95% CI 0.169–0.827); chronotype is not randomized and residual confounding and selection remain possible.

**Source-quality check:** In the paper's RT-qPCR methods, the printed ACTB and REVERBA forward primers are identical, as are the printed reverse primers. This could be a transcription error; the published text alone does not establish what was actually assayed. Because ACTB is described as the normalization reference, the REVERBA assay and its contribution to the eight-gene PCA require clarification from the authors before relying on those specific results. It does not, by itself, prove the reported CLOCK measurement is invalid. We have not sent a message or independently verified primer specificity.

**Data access and genetic-score feasibility:** The article's data-availability statement says underlying data are available on request but are not publicly available due to privacy restrictions. We have not established whether linked genome-wide genotypes exist, whether this project can access the data, associated cost, marker quality, participant overlap or consent for scoring. Thus it is **not an established free genetic-score validation dataset**. The public aggregate paper provides useful motivation for a circadian–immune hypothesis; it does not validate a score, perform a single-cell analysis, or establish a SNP-to-gene mechanism. The sample-size discrepancy, assay reporting question and small robust group limit any future reuse.

For a follow-up, seek independently collected, adequately powered cohorts with repeated timed biosampling, cell-type-resolved expression, standardized insomnia and FI/FFS phenotypes, verified genotype and consent. Prespecify analyses separating sleep and fatigue overlap, and test genetics-to-expression links using appropriately harmonized QTLs and colocalization before interpreting variant mechanisms. Such work would complement, not replace, independent individual-level score validation.
