# Sleep and frailty: what clinical risk studies add

These studies complement the project's genetic correlations; they do not measure the predictive performance of its candidate variants or establish that changing sleep will prevent frailty. Effect estimates use different sleep exposures and frailty instruments and should not be directly compared as if they were the same outcome.

## Prospective evidence

- **A frailty index with insomnia removed:** In the China Kadoorie Biobank, 23,847 adults aged 30–79 who were not frail at baseline were followed for a median of 8 years. Among participants prefrail at baseline, reported insomnia at baseline was associated with subsequent frailty (prevalence ratio 1.53, 95% CI 1.23–1.89). The authors removed insomnia symptoms from their 27-item FI, an especially relevant design choice for our concern about overlapping phenotype definitions. Adjustment included demographic and behavioral variables; additional disease and medication adjustments were a sensitivity analysis. Selection into the resurvey and residual confounding remain possible. [Zhu et al., 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-022-02557-0).
- **Physical frailty and sleep duration:** A Mexican rural cohort followed 309 initially non-frail adults for about 4.4 years, using a Fried-based frailty phenotype. Relative to self-reported 7–8 hours, ≤5 hours was associated with incident frailty (adjusted RR 1.80, 95% CI 1.04–3.11), and ≥9 hours with RR 1.69 (1.10–2.58). The study's sleep-complaint measure was not clearly associated after adjustment (RR 1.41, 0.94–2.12). Sleep duration and insomnia are different exposures, and sample size, attrition, and self-report limit precision and generalizability. [Rural Frailty Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8207661/).
- **Objective sleep and physical frailty:** In 615 US older adults, higher actigraphy-measured fragmentation was associated with frailty at 5 years (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.02–2.84 per 10-unit increase), using a modified Fried measure and adjusting for baseline frailty and other factors. This is a joint frailty/mortality outcome analysis, not an insomnia diagnosis or a personalized risk estimate. [Study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33406254/).
- **Direction may run both ways:** In a two-year Japanese study of 3,844 older adults using the Athens insomnia scale and Kihon checklist, sleep-to-frailty and frailty-to-insomnia standardized cross-lagged coefficients were 0.076 (95% CI 0.045–0.107) and 0.074 (0.044–0.104), respectively. These are standardized model coefficients, not risk ratios, and do not prove either causal direction. [Nemoto et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167494321001825).

## What the pooled number means

A meta-analysis of 12 studies (16,895 participants) reported a pooled insomnia–frailty OR of 1.95 (95% CI 1.52–2.41), but all included studies were cross-sectional and heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 80%). Its Fried-phenotype subgroup pooled OR was 1.53 (1.29–1.81); the 'other instruments' subgroup was 2.29 (1.34–3.92), with no statistically clear subgroup difference (p = 0.16). The latter is a mixed group, not a direct FI-vs-FFS comparison. These are odds of concurrent frailty, not the probability of incident frailty. [Wen et al.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/brb3.2793).

## Implications for the project

The Chinese study shows that an insomnia-excluded FI is feasible and gives a useful observational sensitivity analysis. Our stronger insomnia–FI than insomnia–FFS genetic correlation raises a targeted question: how much reflects the broad deficit-accumulation construct, direct inclusion of insomnia or fatigue, measurement differences, or genetic biology? It does not distinguish these possibilities. The best next analysis is a matched, same-cohort comparison of original FI, FI excluding sleep items, FI excluding sleep and fatigue, and FFS, with identical participants, covariates, follow-up and external validation. Report each definition and pairwise uncertainty; test potential mediators separately rather than presenting observational associations as mechanisms. A sleep-excluded FI GWAS, if accessible, would allow a corresponding LDSC sensitivity analysis; it cannot be reconstructed by subtracting an item from existing summary statistics.

A published NHANES CKD analysis illustrates why this sensitivity matters: its 49-item FI explicitly includes trouble sleeping or sleeping too much and fatigue, while its exposure asks whether participants ever told a health professional about sleep trouble. The questions are not identical, but overlap can contribute to their association. See the [item-level review](stage6_public_nhanes_sleep_frailty_prior_studies.md).
