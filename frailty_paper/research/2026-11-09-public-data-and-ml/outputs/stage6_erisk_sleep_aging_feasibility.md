# E-Risk sleep and aging: no-fee, managed-access lead

Checked 29 October 2026. [Whitman et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13370888/) is a preprint, not yet peer reviewed. It reports a sleep-quality and aging-biomarker analysis in the Environmental Risk (E-Risk) twin cohort. The study reports 1,657 participants aged 18 in the sleep-quality/DunedinPACE analysis. Sleep quality was measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index; DunedinPACE was derived from blood DNA methylation. These are not a clinical insomnia diagnosis and a Fried frailty score or cumulative-deficit frailty index. This cohort is potentially useful for exploratory early-life sleep and biological-aging analyses, but its age and outcomes make it a poor direct test of later-life frailty prediction. Sample size refers to the reported phenotype analysis, not a verified overlap with project-score genotypes.

The [E-Risk access page](https://www.eriskstudy.com/data-access/) states that data access is free for eligible university or research-organization researchers, including students sponsored by an academic supervisor, through a managed process. It lists sleep-relevant and biomarker data, including precomputed polygenic scores, but does not establish that our specific 15 or 22 genotypes, their QC or genotype-phenotype linkage are available. The [SSGAC E-Risk access description](https://www.thessgac.org/data-access-procedures) describes a concept proposal, institutional ethics and secure access; exact present-day requirements and any indirect computing/travel costs should be checked with the provider. A published [E-Risk methylation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6084815/) confirms SNP-array genotypes were used for zygosity QC, not that those genotypes or these exact variants can be supplied to this project. Existing generic polygenic scores cannot reconstruct our score. The cohort's related participants require appropriate family-aware inference. The E-Risk website says the age-30 wave is expected by the end of February 2027; that is a projected availability date, not an accessible dataset or a verified frailty assessment.

The paper reports that poorer sleep and faster methylation aging were correlated, but its within-twin comparisons were uncertain; for identical twins it reports a standardized association of 0.02 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.09). The association's attenuation does not prove that genetics caused it, because the comparisons may have limited power and other differences. In a separate MR analysis the authors reported a self-reported-insomnia estimate of −0.09 (95% CI −0.36 to 0.18) for an MRI-derived aging biomarker, using a UK Biobank split without imaging overlap between the insomnia and biomarker GWAS as described by the authors. Their diagnosed sleep-disorder analyses used different, broader phenotypes and gave different estimates; both rely on MR assumptions and sensitivity to variant selection. These analyses concern aging biomarkers, not FI or Fried frailty, and do not refute or validate our candidate scores. They support reporting phenotype definitions and causal uncertainty in the mentor background.

No application or participant-level analysis was made. For any future request, first verify eligibility, availability and permission for linked 15/22 exact allele dosages, the sleep-quality and biological-aging records, overlap with selection cohorts, and whether suitable physical-frailty measures exist. Because of participant age, outcome mismatch, relatedness and limited sample size, prioritize older cohorts with verified FI or physical outcomes for primary score validation. The project's existing [NCDS](stage6_ncds_sleep_frailty_genetics_feasibility.md) and [NHATS](stage6_nhats_genetic_validation_feasibility.md) reviews cover more directly relevant but controlled-access options.
