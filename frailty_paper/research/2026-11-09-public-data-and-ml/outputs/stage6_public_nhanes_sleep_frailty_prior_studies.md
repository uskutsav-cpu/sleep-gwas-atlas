# Public NHANES sleep–frailty studies: context for validation

Two prior studies using public NHANES data provide phenotype-level context, not validation of either project genetic score.

- [Baniak et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30270714/) studied 3,632 community-dwelling adults older than 60 in NHANES 2011–2014, using self-reported sleep duration and a Fried phenotype adapted to NHANES. They reported an adjusted odds ratio of 2.86 (95% CI 1.09–7.50) for frailty among those reporting at least 10 hours of sleep versus 7–9 hours. This cross-sectional finding concerns sleep duration, not an insomnia diagnosis; the adapted phenotype is not necessarily equivalent to Ye's FFS. The precision is limited and the result does not establish a causal effect or genetic mechanism.
- [Zhang et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869335/) studied 5,211 people with chronic kidney disease in NHANES 2005–2018, using a 49-deficit FI and a report of having told a health professional of trouble sleeping. They reported an adjusted frailty odds ratio of 2.572 (95% CI 2.102–3.147) for that sleep measure versus no report, including sleep duration and the listed covariates. The cohort is selected for kidney disease, and the design is cross-sectional; the exposure is not a clinical insomnia diagnosis. **The [published FI item list](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC11869335.1/IRNF_A_2471008_SM6060.docx) includes “trouble sleeping or sleeping too much” and “feeling tired or having little energy.”** These overlap conceptually with sleep health, although the first item is not identical to the exposure question about ever telling a health professional. This is not a sleep-item-excluded FI analysis, and overlap may contribute to the association; its magnitude cannot be determined from this paper alone. Higher odds do not by themselves establish a temporal or causal relationship.

Both studies include NHANES 2013–2014, the cycle in the project's [sleep-question and grip analysis](stage6_nhanes_sleep_grip_review.md). They are therefore not independent replications of that analysis, and their different sleep and frailty definitions mean their effect estimates are not directly comparable with the grip estimate. These reports do not provide linked public genotypes or test project variants, FI/FFS genetic weights, or individual score prediction. More generally, a phenotypic association does not establish a shared genetic risk.

For a stronger phenotype sensitivity analysis, use the published 49-item scoring specification to predefine a sleep-excluded version and its missing-data threshold before comparing associations; consider fatigue removal as a separate sensitivity analysis. FI construction, survey design and selection need explicit verification; an altered index would need its own evaluation. This could address outcome overlap, but it would still not replace an independent genotype–outcome validation.
