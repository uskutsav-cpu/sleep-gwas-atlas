# Prior NCDS evidence for the 34-item frailty index

[Rogers and colleagues](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article/190/8/1550/6140871) assessed a 34-deficit index at age 50 in 8,711 NCDS participants with at least 30 observed deficits and followed mortality through 2016, observing 209 deaths. Compared with the lowest FI quintile, the highest had a sex-adjusted mortality hazard ratio of **4.07** (95% CI 2.64–6.25). The paper reports a sex-adjusted hazard ratio of **1.48** (95% CI 1.32–1.67) per increase in FI quintile. These are associations of an observed frailty measure with mortality, not the performance of a genetic score, and they do not establish causation or clinical utility in another cohort.

The authors state that FI–mortality associations remained stable when individual items or domains were removed one at a time. They report domain-removal estimates spanning 1.38 to 1.50 per quintile, with cognitive-function and comorbidity removal as the stated endpoints. Because removal changes the index and can change quintile membership, these numbers should not be interpreted as a paired estimate of the causal contribution of any domain, or as the effect of jointly excluding all sleep and fatigue items. The accessible article text does not give the exact sleep-domain-removal result; its referenced web figure was not retrieved in this check. Do not invent a sleep-specific hazard ratio or claim that the study validated our modified FI.

This is relevant prior outcome evidence when choosing among NCDS phenotypes: the separately published [30-item index](ncds_alternative_30_item_fi_review.md) has authors-reported validity limitations and includes the sleep-maintenance question. The two indices use different items and scoring; observational FI–mortality results cannot be used to rank our FFS- and FI-derived genetic prototypes. The 34-item FI itself directly includes sleep latency and waking rested, plus four fatigue items; a sleep-excluded or sleep-and-fatigue-excluded index is a new construct needing its own evaluation.

The published 8,711 is the phenotype-analysis count, not the intersection with the 6,396 people in the later documented genetic release. The original paper notes that survey participants without enough data for the FI had less favorable socioeconomic circumstances, highlighting selection concerns. Exact linkage, marker coverage and independence of discovery/selection cohorts still need assessment.
