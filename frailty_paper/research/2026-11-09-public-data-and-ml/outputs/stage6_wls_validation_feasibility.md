# Wisconsin Longitudinal Study: linked-genotype and insomnia-score lead

The [Wisconsin Longitudinal Study (WLS)](https://researchers.wls.wisc.edu/front-page/data/) is a distinct older-adult US cohort with longitudinal surveys and genome-wide genotyping. Its data page describes approximately 9,000 people with second-wave genetic data, imputed to 1000 Genomes Phase 3. The study requires an application for linked genomic and survey data; the page does not establish the exact genotype–frailty–sleep intersection, project-marker coverage, current cost, or independence from all source participants and relatives. Its graduate cohort is selected from Wisconsin high-school graduates and is not representative of all older US adults; siblings require careful handling.

Published work separately used a [four-component physical-frailty composite in WLS graduate and sibling samples](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5482292/) and studied [sleep quality alongside a neuroticism polygenic score in 4,973 WLS participants](https://doi.org/10.1111/gbb.12644). The frailty paper calls its composite an index, but it sums standardized walking speed, grip strength, BMI and fatigue; it is **not** a deficit-accumulation FI and does not implement the complete five-component Fried phenotype. WLS fatigue was derived from two past-week CES-D items about effort and difficulty getting going, whereas the sleep paper assessed sleep measures in 2003–2005; the physical composite was assessed in 2011. These are separate published analyses and sample counts, not a verified intersection with each other or with either project score. Item content, wave timing, permissible linkage and missingness need further checking for any proposed analysis. A sleep-quality measure is not necessarily an insomnia diagnosis.

The physical-frailty paper reports 4,138 WLS graduates and 1,696 selected siblings with complete frailty and personality measures. They are not a verified genetic-and-sleep-and-frailty intersection, and the two samples include family members; a combined analysis needs relatedness handling rather than treating all records as independent. A comparison with the project's deficit FI would require establishing a separately constructed WLS deficit-accumulation index and its items. Comparing only this published composite to the discovery FFS would be a related physical-frailty analysis, not an equivalent FFS replication.

## Published sleep and physical-frailty item comparison

The WLS [sleep-score paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/gbb.12644) describes a six-month sleep-trouble screening question, followed for those reporting trouble by frequency and discomfort questions; the latter were summed to 0–6, with non-reporters coded zero. This measures reported sleeping difficulty and its discomfort, not a clinical insomnia diagnosis. The [frailty paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5482292/) uses past-week effort and difficulty-getting-going items as its fatigue component, together with measured walk, grip and BMI. The quoted constructs are not the same direct question, but sleep difficulty and fatigue can be related; these distinct measures do not establish independence of the constructs or lack of confounding. The sleep paper's main WLS measures were from 2003–2005; the frailty composite was assessed in 2011, so a future linked analysis could examine earlier reported sleep versus later physical vulnerability if individual dates, eligible participants and pre-existing outcomes permit. That does not by itself establish causation.

The sleep-score paper excluded siblings and its 4,973-person sample had a separate neuroticism score, sleep and demographic measures. The frailty paper included both graduates and siblings, with different eligibility. Neither number establishes how many have a usable WLS repository insomnia score or the 15/22 candidate genotypes together with the 2011 outcome. A future analysis must inspect item availability and linkage under the WLS agreement, specify the timing and missing-data rules, and account for family relatedness. An exhaustion-excluded physical-composite sensitivity would be a new adapted measure, not an automatically validated Fried score.

WLS also lists [Polygenic Index Repository version 2](https://researchers.wls.wisc.edu/front-page/data/) as available through its genomic-data process. The updated repository paper's supplementary table assigns WLS the broad `INSOMNIA1` score, alongside NCDS; the actual current WLS score file and exact linkage terms remain unverified. This score would be an informative separate baseline, **not** either of our 15- or 22-marker scores. Those scores require the relevant individual genotypes and allele coding, not an unrelated precomputed score.

The [WLS non-public-data instructions](https://researchers.wls.wisc.edu/front-page/data/how-to-request-non-public-data/) require a proposal, relevant CVs, IRB documentation, approval and an institutional data-use agreement for genomic data. Costs were not stated in the checked instructions and are unverified. Its data page explicitly prohibits uploading WLS data to public LLMs or AI platforms and states that research involving the human-subject data should be submitted for IRB review. Respect that policy in any future workflow. No WLS participant data were downloaded or uploaded, and no application or external contact was made.
