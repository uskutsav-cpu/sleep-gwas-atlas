# Multi-score frailty prediction: implications for the proposed ML analysis

A [Flint et al. preprint, version 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38853841/), evaluates 26 trait polygenic scores jointly against FI in ELSA and follows selected scores in the Lothian Birth Cohort 1936 (LBC1936). This is a useful precedent for a multivariate genetic model, but it is not a validation of this project's 15- or 22-marker prototypes. The preprint has not been peer reviewed. Its publicly readable results do not provide the linked individual-level data needed to rerun our scores.

The authors report ELSA n = 5,955, 62 FI deficits, and repeated cross-validation of an elastic-net model. Their abstract reports up to 4.7% variance explained for the multi-score approach versus 2.5% for the best single-score model. The results are conditional on the cohort, outcome, covariates, construction and evaluation choices; they are not a percentage increase in personal risk, clinical accuracy, or proof that the added scores would improve a new population's predictions. The paper reports 30 FI deficits in LBC1936, so the outcome is related but not identical. Both samples were restricted to European ancestry.

A key evaluation detail needs clarification: the text describes applying the ELSA model in LBC1936, but also says it explored ten penalty values, selected the lowest-error model and extracted coefficients in LBC1936. Without clearer code or held-out predictions, one should not assume every reported LBC result is from a completely frozen ELSA model tested without target-cohort tuning. Bootstrap coefficient stability does not itself provide independent predictive validation. The paper reports longitudinal association and replication analyses, which should be distinguished from evaluating a frozen prediction model.

For the project, this suggests a design if an eligible public cohort becomes available: freeze candidate scores and all transformations; separate tuning and evaluation; compare age/sex/ancestry covariates, each existing score, and a multi-score elastic-net model in the same participants; report held-out incremental R² for continuous FI or discrimination and calibration for a predefined binary outcome. Evaluate FFS separately, assess sleep-item exclusion, and predefine handling of correlated scores, missing alleles, ancestry, family relationships and overlap. The study does not establish that its chosen predictors include an insomnia score or that combining them will improve the project's outcomes. The current lack of suitable linked public genotype–phenotype data prevents executing this evaluation.

Source: manuscript version 2 public archive text `work/lothian_multiprs_preprint.txt`; original [archived manuscript](https://pmc-oa-opendata.s3.amazonaws.com/PMC11160845.2/PMC11160845.2.txt). This is a methodological comparison, not a new analysis of its participant data.

## Related 2026 multi-omics preprint

A separate [Flint et al. 2026 preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.06.30.26356940v1) uses multiple frailty definitions, including FI, FFS and domain-specific factors, to investigate chronic pain and rheumatoid arthritis through genetic association and molecular analyses. It describes genotyped older-adult samples in LBC1936 and PISA. The study reports its generated data are available on reasonable request; this is not an open individual-level download and neither cost nor project-specific data eligibility has been established. No request was made.

The preprint is a useful comparison for modeling frailty as several related constructs and for integrating genetic loci with molecular information. Its main exposures are chronic pain and rheumatoid arthritis, not insomnia; its nominated pathways are not independent evidence of an insomnia–frailty mechanism. Both PISA and LBC1936 have already been considered for this project, and phenotype overlap, genomic selection, allele coding, access and outcome definitions remain necessary checks. Its causal language depends on Mendelian-randomization assumptions, and the paper has not been peer reviewed. It does not validate either project score, and applying its methods to our sleep data would require separate analyses rather than treating its reported results as ours.
