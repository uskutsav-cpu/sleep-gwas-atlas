# Sleep and frailty risk beyond the genetic correlations

## Insomnia measured against an FI without the insomnia item

[Zhu et al. (2022)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12916-022-02557-0) studied 23,847 China Kadoorie Biobank participants aged 30–79 who completed baseline and a second resurvey, with median 8.0 years between surveys. The investigators removed insomnia symptoms from their frailty index, leaving 27 items, and classified people as robust, prefrail or frail. This is a useful example of reducing direct measurement overlap, although it is a different FI and does not establish that all related symptoms or indirect overlap were removed.

Among participants prefrail at baseline, the paper reports that baseline questionnaire-defined insomnia disorder was associated with a **prevalence ratio of 1.53 (95% CI 1.23–1.89)** for being frail at the second survey. The analysis compared those whose prefrail status worsened with those who remained prefrail, as described in the methods; it adjusted for sex, age, urban/rural study area, education, smoking and alcohol. The paper's insomnia definition combined symptom, daytime dysfunction and sleep-medication questions, so it is not interchangeable with the project's UKB self-report or registry insomnia phenotypes. The ratio is a relative association in this selected analysis, not a person's absolute risk and not a genetic risk estimate.

This was observational. Participants had to survive and participate in both surveys, and the study excluded baseline frailty and people with missing FI variables; selection and attrition may affect results. Excluding baseline frailty cannot eliminate reverse causation or residual confounding. The authors also examined sleep maintained at both baseline and resurvey; because the later sleep measure is concurrent with the follow-up frailty assessment, that comparison should not be presented as a baseline prediction or as proof that maintaining sleep prevents frailty. The FI's disease-history ascertainment differed between surveys. No FI GWAS with the insomnia item removed was performed for the present project, and this study's observational association cannot be substituted for the project's LDSC genetic correlations.

## An objective-sleep and physical-frailty example

In the [National Social Life, Health and Aging Project sleep study](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/7/zsab003/6066553), 615 community-dwelling older adults who were not frail at baseline were followed for approximately five years. Using actigraphy and a modified five-component Fried-style outcome, the authors reported an adjusted **OR 1.70 (95% CI 1.02–2.84) per 10-unit increase in sleep fragmentation** for later frailty, controlling for baseline frailty category, age, sex, education, BMI and sleep time preference. This uses sleep fragmentation, not a diagnosis of insomnia, and the outcome is a modified Fried measure rather than the exact Ye GWAS FFS.

The confidence interval is wide. The authors report attrition, modified frailty measurements and mean replacement for missing follow-up frailty data among limitations; unmeasured health conditions and other confounding remain possible. This association does not establish that improving sleep prevents frailty, a causal gene, or score performance.

## A check on temporal direction

In a two-year [Japanese longitudinal study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34564037/) of 3,844 community-dwelling older adults, the authors used the Athens Insomnia Scale, Kihon frailty checklist and a cross-lagged model. They reported standardized coefficients of **0.076 (95% CI 0.045–0.107)** for baseline insomnia symptoms predicting later frailty and **0.074 (0.044–0.104)** for baseline frailty predicting later insomnia symptoms. These are model coefficients, not relative or absolute risks. The [article's participant flow](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167494321001825) reports that 3,844 of 4,832 eligible respondents completed follow-up (79.6%); the study excluded people receiving long-term care at baseline. Selection, attrition, confounding and measure overlap therefore matter. Although the authors discussed sex-specific patterns, this is not evidence that an intervention would work differently by sex or proof of either causal direction.

The findings are not uniform across definitions and models. A separate [UK Biobank study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11911041/) followed 3,192 participants aged 55–70 at baseline across three assessment waves and used random-intercept cross-lagged models of multidimensional frailty and sleep quality. Its abstract reports reciprocal associations for social frailty but not for its physical, psychological or cognitive frailty measures. The study's physical measure and broad sleep-quality construct are not the Ye FFS and clinical-insomnia GWAS; lack of statistical significance does not prove no association. Its repeat-visit selection and potential overlap with the project's UK Biobank GWAS also limit generalization and independence.

The UKB paper's methods make the construct difference explicit: its **0–11 physical-frailty score** combines grip strength, exhaustion, physical activity, perceived health, walking, hearing and vision; it did not include weight loss or balance. Its **0–6 healthy-sleep score** combines ease of getting up, chronotype, sleep duration, insomnia symptoms, snoring and daytime sleepiness. Higher values mean more physical frailty for the former and better sleep for the latter. Thus its result cannot be described as a test of the project's five-domain FFS against insomnia alone; the physical score contains exhaustion and the sleep score includes daytime sleepiness, so related content may remain. These methods were checked against the paper's full text, retrieved from Europe PMC (local XML SHA-256 `346365ca7d10c013689227f9972770cc26bf51f27a11518e6fbe42835ec42eac`).

These analyses answer different questions: the Japanese study examined associations between measurements across two occasions, whereas the UKB random-intercept models aim to separate stable between-person differences from within-person changes. Neither establishes an intervention effect, and their differing populations, frailty and sleep measures, sampling and modeling prevent a simple direct comparison of effect sizes. Together they motivate testing both directions and multiple frailty definitions in a prospective study, alongside the sleep-item-excluded FI sensitivity.

## How this informs the project

The two studies motivate examining measured sleep and frailty in addition to GWAS statistics. They provide examples of a sleep-item-excluded FI and objective sleep assessment, but neither directly tests why the genetic correlation differs for this project's FI and FFS. The most informative genetic comparison remains to construct original and sleep/fatigue-excluded FI in the **same** genotyped participants and repeat the GWAS with matched analytic choices, followed by appropriately paired LDSC comparisons. The [feasibility review](sleep_excluded_frailty_gwas_feasibility.md) describes the necessary data and remaining access issues.
