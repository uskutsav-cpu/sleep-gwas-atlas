# Insomnia and frailty: findings and next steps for discussion

**Working synthesis, 28 October 2026.** This project asks whether insomnia and frailty share genetic risk, which biological processes may explain that overlap, and whether genetic information can eventually improve frailty prediction. We have useful population-level and molecular clues, including an exploratory signal in the FOXP2 region. We have **not** demonstrated a causal gene, shown that insomnia causes frailty, or independently validated the project's genetic scores. All analyses described here are research findings, not individual health predictions.

## Further findings and validation status (29 October 2026 UTC)

The independent participant-level evaluation of the project’s 15-marker weighted prototypes and 22-marker count is **still outstanding**. No public linked genotype–insomnia–frailty dataset with adequate marker coverage, phenotype definitions, sample size and documented non-overlap has been established. The no-fee NHATS route is controlled access, and its actual marker coverage, linkage and approval remain unverified. A reference-panel screen found nearby generic array markers with r² ≥ 0.8 for 7/15 and 6/22 candidates; this is not NHATS imputation quality or score validation. [Coverage and limitations](stage6_gsa_reference_tag_screen_review.md).

A post hoc paired NHANES comparison on fraction-of-items scales found uncertain differences between sleep-trouble associations for the adapted FI and a five-domain physical-frailty count in both cycles. The scales are not equivalent clinical severity measures, and this does not compare predictive accuracy or genetic scores. [Paired analysis](stage6_nhanes_fi_ffs_comparison_review.md).

The published PISA frailty index includes several sleep-related items. PISA also has linked genotype–FI research and an onsite sleep-monitoring subset, but access is by request and fees, linkage and score-marker coverage remain unverified. This underscores the need for a sleep-excluded FI sensitivity, not a completed validation. [PISA audit](stage6_pisa_sleep_frailty_validation_feasibility.md).

Two new CHARLS checks illustrate why phenotype definitions matter. A 2026 sleep-duration study’s 44-item FI explicitly includes poor sleep quality and effort; a sleep-excluded sensitivity was not documented in the checked supplement. Another publicly released CHARLS analysis file reproduced the reported short-sleep versus 6–8-hour psychological-frailty association (OR 1.27), with an estimate of 1.62 in a same-sample sensitivity without depression-score adjustment. These are observational analyses, use related CHARLS data and are neither independent replication of each other nor genetic score tests. [44-item audit](stage6_charls_chen2026_sleep_overlap_audit.md); [reproduction](stage6_charls_pfi_public_reproduction_review.md).

A public whole-blood GENOA methylation-QTL scan identified an exploratory association between the C/T contrast at rs11636381 and a nearby CpG, cg03789871. It is postselected and has not been tied to insomnia or frailty, a particular causal gene or a cell type. A GoDMC query did not give an evaluable same-CpG replication; its returned data were accessed over unauthenticated HTTP. The separate nearby LOXL1-annotated frailty-associated CpG is outside GENOA’s test window for this variant, so proximity is not evidence of a molecular link. [GENOA evidence](stage6_genoa_rs11636381_mqtl_review.md); [cross-study comparison](stage6_genoa_godmc_rs11636381_review.md).

The Spanish [GCAT PolyGenie analysis](stage6_polygenie_sleep_frailty_review.md) tests broad published FFS and insomnia scores against diagnoses in a distinct named cohort; it does not test our prototypes. The nominal FFS-score association with broad sleep-disorder diagnosis is postselected and does not pass a within-domain correction. Its displayed “domain-wise” threshold counts domains rather than outcomes, so consult the [multiple-testing audit](stage6_polygenie_multiple_testing_audit.md) before interpreting labels.

A Canadian ordinal-frailty GWAS is a useful external-cohort lead, but the checked publication provides selected results without the signed effect estimates required for our score analysis. A supplementary variant appears under two chromosome labels, so its identity requires correction before use. [Source audit](stage6_borhan_frailty_selected_results_audit.md).

## Why compare FI and FFS?

Frailty captures vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, but its measurement affects the question. A **frailty index (FI)** is the proportion of a specified set of health deficits present in a person; it includes physical, clinical and sometimes psychological items. The **Fried frailty score (FFS)** counts five physical features: weakness, slowness, exhaustion, low activity and unintentional weight loss. FI and FFS overlap but are not interchangeable. The FI used in one of our main GWAS includes insomnia and tiredness; FFS includes exhaustion. Consequently, genetic overlap with insomnia can partly reflect how phenotypes are defined, alongside genuine shared biology and other influences.

The original Atkins supplement now confirms the published UKB FI includes insomnia and codes the intermediate fatigue response as 0.5; its listed 44 TwinGene items have no explicitly named sleep or fatigue item. The original executed code remains unaudited, and the two cohorts differ in more than sleep content. [Source crosswalk](fi_ffs_component_crosswalk.md).

Our linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) estimated genome-wide genetic correlation **r_g=0.757** (SE 0.021) between the Atkins FI and Ye FFS GWAS. A separate FI/modified physical-frailty comparison from Mak's studies gave r_g=0.796 (SE 0.012). These are correlations of genetic effects across the genome, not the proportion of patients or causal variants shared, and the GWAS samples overlap or may overlap. In a separate clinical cohort, the FI and 0–5 Fried count had a Spearman correlation of approximately 0.54, illustrating that person-level agreement answers a different question. [FI/FFS comparison](fi_ffs_component_crosswalk.md); [same-cohort clinical evidence](fi_ffs_same_cohort_comparison.md).

## Prior studies and motivation

[Song et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnagi.2024.1358996/full) reported 124 candidate variants from an insomnia–FI cross-trait analysis. Their source traits include UK Biobank data, and the FI includes sleep-related items. Our follow-up evaluates these and other candidates against multiple sleep and frailty measures; its candidate screens are **postselected**. They motivate specific follow-up, not independent proof that all candidates are causal for both traits.

Using three insomnia sources, our matched LDSC comparisons consistently gave a higher insomnia correlation with FI than with FFS. For the original FI/FFS comparison, the estimates were approximately **0.640 versus 0.483** for UK Biobank insomnia symptoms, **0.426 versus 0.245** for FinnGen clinical insomnia, and **0.549 versus 0.398** for MVP clinical insomnia. Paired block-jackknife comparisons support differences under the LDSC model, but source traits, sample overlap, ascertainment and reference LD differ. We cannot attribute the difference solely to an insomnia question, fatigue or a biological pathway. The corresponding [paired uncertainty audit](insomnia_frailty_paired_uncertainty_audit.md) explains why comparing separate p-values is insufficient.

A valuable sensitivity target is the **modified 41-item FI** reported by [Mak et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.13868): it excludes eight mental-wellbeing items, including insomnia and fatigue. Its full genome-wide results have not been located in the public releases inspected. Even if obtained, this would test a broader eight-item construct change rather than isolate insomnia alone. A stronger design would recompute the same FI in the same participants, first excluding insomnia and then excluding insomnia plus fatigue, and rerun the GWAS. [Source audit](mak2023_sleep_excluded_fi_lead.md).

The mentor's reference to **“Zunpeng sources” remains unresolved**. A possible lead is Zunpeng Liu’s publicly described brain-cell epigenomic atlas; a [conditional peak-overlap screen](stage6_liu_brain_ccre_candidate_review.md) found four candidate positions in subtype-labelled peaks, but these are not eQTLs, causal genes, sleep–frailty evidence or independent score validation. Its source-file coordinate assumptions and mentor attribution require confirmation.

## Shared risk: what the data actually support

The strongest regional follow-up so far is **FOXP2-region** insomnia and hospital frailty. In a postselected 1-Mb window, SuSiE-coloc using gnomAD non-Finnish European LD for MVP insomnia and Finnish LD for FinnGen HFRS included 1,257 aligned variants. At one stated prior, a disease-signal pair had **PP.H4=0.979**. An expanded, post hoc shared-prior sensitivity gave PP.H4 from 0.045 to 0.996, including 0.322 at p12=1e-7; the appropriate prior is not established. This is a regional hypothesis, and the evidence does not establish a shared causal variant or gene. MVP and FinnGen are named separate cohorts, but complete participant and relatedness non-overlap is unverified; external LD, postselection, a narrower window and HFRS's different clinical definition limit the inference. [Disease-region review](stage6_foxp2_gnomad_matched_coloc_review.md).

An important caution for the FI itself: rs12536335 has opposite estimated UK Biobank and TwinGene FI effects under an inferred, not author-confirmed TwinGene allele-direction adapter. A regional check finds the same pattern among correlated high-LD markers, but the smaller TwinGene fit yields no credible set; this is not an independently validated biological difference. [FI cohort review](stage6_foxp2_fi_cohort_regional_review.md).

A separately reported HRS–ELSA FI locus, rs7134291 near GRIN2B, had imprecise follow-up estimates in MVP insomnia and FinnGen hospital frailty; this does not test or validate the project scores. [Independent FI-locus context](stage6_hrs_elsa_grin2b_cross_trait_review.md).

The gene and cell question remains open. An expanded pituitary FOXP2 eQTL analysis included all four unique variants in one published molecular credible set. At the illustrated prior, it favored **distinct** pituitary-expression versus insomnia and HFRS signals. This does not exclude FOXP2 involvement elsewhere, and it is sensitive to sample size, LD and assumptions. A brain excitatory-neuron expression comparison could not produce an insomnia colocalization posterior because the insomnia fit returned no credible set on the shared support. [Pituitary analysis](stage6_foxp2_pituitary_indel_coloc_review.md); [brain analysis](stage6_foxp2_brain_mvp_pair_review.md).

A musculoskeletal factor study reported an association at the same FOXP2-region marker. Our three pairwise analyses used only 790 shared variants: the new support retained **6 of 18 variants**, representing about **48% of the original insomnia credible set's component mass**. Although their model outputs were high, this loss of coverage, the factor's Genomic SEM effective sample size and overlapping inputs make the apparent sharing too fragile to establish a three-trait causal signal. [Coverage and analysis](stage6_gong_foxp2_signal_sharing_review.md).

At **RBM6**, brain mural-cell expression-model associations were stronger for some frailty measures than for clinical insomnia or HFRS; they are uncalibrated, postselected TWAS results, not mediation evidence. A musculoskeletal paper reports RBM6 expression colocalizations under several immune-cell labels, but its stated eQTL source and cell labels are not sufficiently consistent to verify their provenance or call them independent single-cell confirmation. [RBM6 TWAS](stage6_psychad_rbm6_twas_review.md); [cell-source audit](stage6_gong2026_musculoskeletal_followup.md).

A later, narrower RBM6-region SuSiE analysis found one modeled insomnia–FI signal pair that favored sharing at one prior, but it used overlapping UK Biobank data, an FI that includes insomnia, and a window that omitted much of an earlier insomnia credible set. HFRS yielded no credible set, and no variant–gene mediation was established. [Regional follow-up](stage6_rbm6_gnomad_coloc_review.md).

An excitatory-neuron RBM6 eQTL analysis produced candidate pairwise overlap with insomnia and FI, but retained only about 23–25% of the earlier insomnia credible-set mass and relied on uncertain effective sample size. It is a hypothesis, not a confirmed gene mechanism. [Brain-expression follow-up](stage6_rbm6_brain_molecular_coloc_review.md).

A separate GTEx bulk-cortex RBM6 eQTL comparison retains all variants in two earlier insomnia and FI credible sets and yields candidate pairwise overlap, but it misses the highest-PIP variant in the published cortex expression credible set (rs10049477, PIP 0.503). The restricted posterior is therefore fragile; an expanded frailty-study analysis recovers that variant and still favors one expression–FI signal pair, although the FFS source lacks allele frequencies and the result remains conditional on external LD and the selected variants. [Expanded analysis](stage6_rbm6_gtex_direct_coloc_review.md). Bulk tissue does not identify the relevant cell, and these results do not validate patient risk prediction. [Cortex coverage and colocalization audit](stage6_rbm6_gtex_cortex_coloc_review.md). A direct 985-variant insomnia–cortex sensitivity analysis has a similar conditional posterior, but still misses the leading published expression variant; it does not independently confirm the hypothesis. [Insomnia sensitivity](stage6_rbm6_gtex_jansen_coloc_review.md). A separate veteran insomnia study includes the leading expression variant; in its expanded analysis the cortex expression and insomnia signals favor distinct variants at the illustrated prior, with substantial prior sensitivity. This weakens a simple shared-RBM6 interpretation without proving the signals are different. [Veteran insomnia follow-up](stage6_rbm6_gtex_mvp_coloc_review.md). The contrast persists when both insomnia studies are refit on the same 960 variants, but that shared set omits the leading published expression variant; phenotype and study differences remain unresolved. [Matched-variant comparison](stage6_rbm6_sleep_matched_review.md).

A [selected-lead Estonian insomnia proxy screen](stage6_estonian_insomnia_proxy_review.md) found three 22-marker candidates with strong European reference LD to a reported Estonian lead, including one in the FOXP2 region; their lead estimates were uncertain. This post hoc locus context does not validate a score or test frailty.

A [public Estonian sleep-diagnosis follow-up](stage6_estonian_sleep_replication_selected_variants.md) includes the MEIS1-region project marker rs113851554: its insomnia effect is directionally positive but uncertain (p = 0.058), while its restless-legs association is much stronger. The postselected single-marker result does not validate the score or a frailty outcome.

At the **MEIS1-region** candidate rs113851554, public polysomnography and clinical studies support association with periodic limb movements and highlight restless legs as an important alternative explanation for part of a sleep association. A clinical insomnia-without-RLS comparison was inconclusive for small effects. These studies measure different phenotypes and some reuse participants; they do not establish a frailty mechanism. [External sleep evidence](stage6_meis1_independent_sleep_phenotype_review.md).

Published gene-model overlap provides additional hypotheses: LRPPRC and CCDC134 appear in selected FI and insomnia TWAS tables, but those tables use related UK Biobank/GTEx data and differing thresholds. A shared gene name is not evidence that the same variant or mechanism drives both outcomes. [Published TWAS comparison](stage3_published_fi_insomnia_twas_review.md).

An additional [TenK10K immune multiome screen](stage6_tenk10k_smad5_molecular_review.md) reports a naïve-CD4 SMAD5 eQTL–chromatin QTL colocalization at a peak overlapping the prior CASCADE signal. A subsequent [exact caQTL lookup](stage6_tenk10k_smad5_variant_caqtl_review.md) found rs7734045 ALT C strongly associated with the same immune-cell peak; cohort overlap, the source correction version and molecular–disease signal sharing remain limitations.

A [locus-wide TenK10K chromatin follow-up](stage6_tenk10k_peak_disease_coloc_review.md) favors distinct FI and peak signals in a single-signal model; the insomnia and physical-frailty comparisons are inconclusive. A frequency-guided sensitivity includes the palindromic top SNP and yields the same qualitative conclusions, though its orientation remains conditional; multivariant modeling and independent data remain needed.

A [restricted-LD SuSiE sensitivity](stage6_tenk10k_fi_susie_ld_review.md) also favors distinct FI and immune-cell peak signals, but the reference covers only part of the locus and is rank deficient. FP and insomnia lack enough retained support for that comparison.

The newly released [skeletal-muscle multiome screen](stage6_varshney_skeletal_muscle_qtl_review.md) places rs10865954 in three molecular expression credible sets and eight project candidates in chromatin credible sets, generally with modest molecular probabilities. None is a demonstrated sleep–frailty colocalization, causal gene or score validation.

## AlphaGenome and AI: how to bridge the work

We used AlphaGenome to score **488 selected alleles** and retained raw responses. The model predicts molecular effects of sequence changes; its predictions are not independent patient-outcome data. Some requested splice outputs were absent for 221 alleles, which must not be encoded as zero. At selected FOXP2 pituitary and SMAD5 immune comparisons, predicted and measured directions or assays do not consistently agree. Differences in assay, tissue and source prevent a simple model-accuracy claim. [Results and coverage](stage5_live_results_summary.md); [track provenance caveat](stage5_alphagenome_track_independence_audit.md).

The current [AlphaGenome terms and provenance notice](ALPHAGENOME_OUTPUT_TERMS_NOTICE.md) matters for this plan: the service terms restrict training variant-effect models similar to AlphaGenome using covered outputs, while the separately displayed output terms describe a broader restriction and exceptions. The applicable terms depend on the output and agreement. To avoid assuming an exception, the proposed models below do not train on API outputs or their derivatives; review terms before any output-derived training or distribution.

A [multi-score FI preprint](stage6_multiprs_frailty_external_study_review.md) provides a relevant elastic-net precedent in ELSA and Lothian. Its Lothian evaluation describes target-cohort penalty selection, so it should not automatically be treated as an untouched test of a frozen model. It does not validate our scores or establish an insomnia-specific improvement.

A practical AI project can be built in stages:

1. **Define a variant–gene–cell–phenotype evidence graph.** Nodes represent harmonized alleles, genes, cell states and traits. Edges hold GWAS statistics, credible-set membership, eQTL/colocalization results and regulatory annotations, with build, ancestry, source, sample overlap, direction and missingness retained. Show AlphaGenome predictions separately as research annotations, outside the training features. Nearest-gene links must be labeled positional; do not treat all edges as causal.
2. **Start with transparent baselines.** Rank candidate variant–gene–cell hypotheses using prespecified, appropriately licensed non-AlphaGenome evidence features; compare against GWAS p-value, nearest-gene and simple logistic/ranking baselines, and check prioritization against independently measured molecular effects. Avoid training on the same sources later presented as validation.
3. **Test embeddings only after the baseline.** Train embeddings using independently sourced, appropriately licensed non-AlphaGenome features to predict a separately measured regulatory or variant–gene outcome, with matched background variants and explicit handling of missing assays. Hold out entire genomic regions or chromosomes—not random variants from the same LD block—and, where possible, independent experiments and donors. Report precision/recall, uncertainty and performance by ancestry, tissue and variant class. Review AlphaGenome results separately after training; an embedding is a representation, not proof of a biological interaction.
4. **Move to phenotype prediction only with eligible outcome data.** Freeze a score and compare it with age/sex/clinical baseline and published genome-wide scores in independent participants. Assess calibration, discrimination and incremental value with paired uncertainty. Public summary statistics and genotype-only reference panels cannot supply those measures. No suitable freely public individual-level target with both the required genotypes and frailty outcomes has been established. An older open Personal Genome Project snapshot has linked records but insufficient established outcome and genotype coverage for this test. [Feasibility check](stage6_pgp_validation_feasibility.md).

## Recommended next steps

1. **Resolve phenotype overlap:** locate full signed results and coding for a sleep/fatigue-excluded FI; if unavailable, state this gap and use HFRS and physical frailty as different-phenotype sensitivity analyses, not replacements for a matched item-removal test.
2. **Strengthen FOXP2 and RBM6:** obtain full variant coverage, matched LD and independent molecular/phenotype datasets; use multisignal coloc, report prior sensitivity and compare variant–gene evidence in relevant brain, muscle and immune cells. Do not infer the relevant cell merely from nearest-gene or model labels.
3. **Investigate MEIS1 phenotype specificity:** where data permit, prespecify analyses with and without restless-legs or movement symptoms; avoid causal mediation claims from adjustment alone and check cohort overlap.
4. **Build and benchmark the evidence graph:** prioritize testable allele–gene–cell hypotheses with suitably licensed non-AlphaGenome features and independent functional assays; review AlphaGenome predictions separately under their applicable terms, and keep the final experimental test separate from model selection.
5. **Freeze and validate a prediction model only if appropriate target data become available.** The 15-marker weighted sums and 22-marker count are different, postselected research prototypes, not calibrated risk tools. The recent FinnGen HFRS weighted-Z test is a summary-level association check, not a measure of patient prediction. [Score test and limitations](stage6_hfrs_sparse_score_summary_review.md). The 15-marker weights also have exploratory summary-statistic associations in two insomnia GWAS, but neither that analysis nor the separate 22-marker count is independently validated for patient prediction. [Score evidence and remaining validation](stage6_score_validation_evidence_summary.md).

**Discussion decision:** Which clinical outcome and frailty definition should be primary, and which "Zunpeng" papers or resources should be incorporated? The public-data analyses can proceed in parallel, but those choices are needed to frame a prospective prediction study and accurately attribute prior work.

### Expanded TenK10K–FI LD sensitivity

An expanded 503-person European reference increased the frequency-screened FI comparison from 1,743 to 3,754 SNPs and recovered the molecular lead. SuSiE-coloc still favored distinct over shared signals (H3 0.9994, H4 0.00049 at illustrative priors), but LD mismatch diagnostics increased and the reference matrix is rank deficient; this remains exploratory biological follow-up, not independent validation. See [expanded sensitivity and limitations](stage6_tenk10k_expanded_fi_susie_review.md).

The public Finnish SISu 4.2 LD service gives r²=0.359 between the two fitted molecular and FI signal hits versus 0.317 in the smaller European panel; the molecular lead and fitted molecular hit have r²=0.994. This checks a few pairwise relationships only and cannot replace a signed LD matrix or independent validation. See [Finnish LD check](stage6_tenk10k_finnish_ld_anchor_review.md).

An additional public NHANES cycle (2011–2012) supports a separate-cycle phenotype follow-up using the same sleep question and grip model. The estimate was +0.19 kg (95% CI −1.43 to 1.80); this is imprecise and does not validate an insomnia–frailty genetic score or full FI/FFS. See [follow-up](stage6_nhanes_2011_sleep_grip_followup.md).

A newly published African-ancestry handgrip GWAS yielded 16 exact coordinate-and-allele matches among the existing 22 candidates. One (rs7731706 G/T) showed nominal p=0.009 for lower grip in the higher-insomnia/frailty-point-estimate direction, but did not pass 0.05/22, has a multiallelic context, and source effect units are unresolved. This is an exploratory cross-population component-phenotype lookup, not independent score validation. [Full follow-up](stage6_hansen_grip_candidate_followup.md).

At the chromosome 5 region, rs7731706 G and rs7734045 C have modest reference LD (r²≈0.13 in 1000 Genomes AFR and 0.14 in EUR); the signed correlations have opposite signs in those pooled panels. These reference observations do not establish shared causal variation, and the AFR panel is not proven representative of the Hansen sample. [Reference comparison](stage6_hansen_chr5_reference_ld_review.md).

The complete older-adult CHARGE gait-speed GWAS was located on NHLBI GRASP and screened: 11 of 22 project candidates matched on rsID and allele pair, and none reached nominal p<0.05; one frequency-discordant multiallelic match is not suitable for direction interpretation. These imprecise, post-selected, component-phenotype associations do not establish null effects or validate an FI/FFS score. [Full gait follow-up](stage6_charge_gait_full_candidate_review.md).

The same public gait GWAS was also screened against the 124 published insomnia–FI candidate rsIDs: 45 had unique source rows, two were nominally associated, and neither passed 0.05/124. This is an rsID-only, post-selection screen with unresolved allele equivalence, not independent validation of the project score. [Broader gait screen](stage6_charge_gait_song124_review.md).

CDC's public NHANES daily accelerometry data supported a two-cycle exploratory test of device-classified sleep-wear time versus grip in older adults: the adjusted estimate per hour was −0.75 kg (95% CI −1.32 to −0.19) in 2011–2012 and −0.22 kg (95% CI −0.89 to +0.45) in 2013–2014. These are not insomnia diagnoses, full frailty measures, genetic-score validation or independent cohorts relative to our earlier NHANES analyses. [Methods and limitations](stage6_nhanes_device_sleep_grip_review.md).

### Additional public-data phenotype analysis: adapted NHANES deficit index

After correcting the survey weights to examination weights, in NHANES participants aged 60+, reported clinician-discussed sleep trouble was associated with a higher 24-item adapted deficit index in both 2011–12 (adjusted difference 0.071, 95% CI 0.051–0.092; n=1,554) and 2013–14 (0.090, 0.064–0.116; n=1,652). The index excludes the direct sleep and fatigue items; removing concentration and psychomotor items as well gave similar associations. A post hoc matched-sample sensitivity removing all six depression-screening items retained positive associations, but the resulting 18-item measure is also unvalidated. This is an exploratory adaptation, not a validated clinical FI, a diagnosed insomnia measure, genetic replication, or causal evidence. See [analysis and caveats](stage6_nhanes_adapted_fi_review.md).

### Independent validation lead: NHATS

NHATS is a promising no-fee controlled-access cohort for a future participant-level evaluation, with 4,006 QC-passing genotyped participants and published insomnia-symptom and physical-frailty measurements. This is not open genotype data; access, wave-level linkage, marker coverage, population overlap and statistical power still need confirmation. The [feasibility review and application-ready outline](stage6_nhats_genetic_validation_feasibility.md) explain the route and restrictions. No score validation has been completed in NHATS.

### Same-participant deficit index and physical component

A post hoc analysis of the same NHANES participants in each cycle found positive sleep-trouble associations with the adapted 24-item deficit index, while estimates for measured grip strength were uncertain. Grip alone is not FFS, and differing significance does not prove a difference between outcomes. The [matched-sample results](stage6_nhanes_same_sample_fi_grip_review.md) help motivate a proper same-cohort FI–FFS comparison but do not supply genetic validation.

### Same-participant FI and physical frailty phenotypes

A post hoc comparison of the adapted deficit index with a five-domain, questionnaire-based modified Fried phenotype in the same NHANES participants found positive age/sex-adjusted associations with reported sleep trouble in both 2011–12 and 2013–14. A four-domain count omitting exhaustion was also positively associated. The physical measure substitutes self-report for measured gait and grip, and its age-specific sedentary threshold has substantial ties; neither phenotype is equivalent to the original discovery FI/FFS. Their coefficients cannot be compared directly, and neither comparison validates genetic scores. [Methods, estimates and limitations](stage6_nhanes_fi_ffs_comparison_review.md).

### Illustration of sleep-item overlap

In a post hoc NHANES comparison using the same people, adding the sleep exposure itself and a fatigue item to the adapted deficit index increased the observed sleep–index association in both cycles. This comparison is intentionally circular: the exposure becomes part of the outcome, so an association can be amplified mechanically. The sleep/fatigue-excluded index still showed an association, but these observations cannot determine how much of the genetic correlation is due to measurement overlap. [Methods and results](stage6_nhanes_sleep_item_overlap_review.md).

### Working evidence-graph artifact

A [first machine-readable evidence graph](stage6_evidence_graph_readme.md) connects the 33 distinct variants across both project lists to positional genes and seven distinct GWAS study/phenotype nodes, retaining allele orientation and source caveats. It now includes cell-resolved skeletal-muscle molecular credible-set edges, but no independent experimental-label edges and does not train an ML model. It provides a reproducible starting point for the proposed non-AlphaGenome benchmark; independent molecular labels and genomic-region holdouts remain necessary.

### Initial ML baseline

A fixed sequence-only logistic-regression baseline was tested on a public neuronal reporter study, with chromosomes 1 and 2 held out. Its AUROC was 0.517 on 161 selected variants, indicating little clear ranking ability in this small exploratory split; average precision was 0.298 versus a 0.199 detected-call prevalence. The labels represent statistical assay detection, not functional ground truth, and the task does not test insomnia–frailty risk. [Methods and limitations](stage6_neuronal_mpra_ml_baseline_review.md).

### Follow-up: clinical symptom summary-statistic QC

A candidate allele-frequency and imputation-field review, and an alternative-frequency sensitivity calculation for the 15-marker exploratory score, are documented in `stage6_mdd_candidate_qc_review.md`. Frequency checks did not reveal large mismatches, and approximate score Z values changed little. These checks do not establish cohort independence or validate either frailty phenotype.

### Published hospital-frailty molecular follow-up

A [cross-reference of published pQTL and eQTL tables](stage6_hfrs_candidate_molecular_published_audit.md) found that the reported MAD1L1 and NCAM1 protein and hospital-frailty signals favored distinct variants under the study's model. A dementia-excluded FOXP2 adrenal eQTL comparison used only one shared SNP, making its apparent sharing non-discriminating. Neither analysis tested insomnia or validated the genetic scores.

### Public longevity candidate follow-up

The 33 distinct project candidates were all matched in a freely available longevity GWAS; none had nominal individual-variant p<0.05. This postselected, aging-outcome check does not exclude effects or validate the scores, and cohort overlap has not been resolved. [Details](stage6_deelen_longevity_candidate_review.md).

A follow-up [combined-variant longevity sensitivity](stage6_deelen_longevity_candidate_review.md) gave approximate Z values between −0.93 and +0.43 across the score-weight choices. These are postselected summary-statistic approximations for longevity, not evidence that the scores predict frailty or that their effects are absent.

A [15-marker follow-up in the African-ancestry grip GWAS](stage6_hansen_15_candidate_review.md) matched 11 markers; none had nominal individual p<0.05. Grip is a component, four markers were unavailable, effect units remain unverified, and this same source was already used for the 22-marker screen. It is not score validation.

### Open Lithuanian older-adult genotype source

The [GEPITEL data audit](stage6_gepitel_public_validation_feasibility.md) found an openly downloadable genotype archive from a study with Fried frailty measurements. Its FAM contains no phenotypes, and its directly genotyped array has no candidate matches on the checked identifiers or coordinates; a separate lean-mass summary workbook contains one 22-marker candidate and a later scan found that all its rows have p < 0.05; it is not a full all-p-value results file. Thus the release is a real public-data lead but does not yet allow score validation.

The GEPITEL LMI workbook has a material [assembly inconsistency](stage6_gepitel_public_validation_feasibility.md): four checked positions match GRCh37 although the article states GRCh38. Resolve this before using its regional or allele associations.

A [public blood-expression source audit](stage6_gse140358_frailty_expression_source_audit.md) found 25 total GEO samples, despite a later paper's description of 25 frail and 25 robust; the metadata use three labels, including four labeled frail. It should not be used as a 50-person independent validation or as evidence of a genetic score mechanism.

A small [public muscle RNA-seq follow-up](stage6_gse144304_muscle_candidate_expression_review.md) found no detectable frail/pre-frail versus fit differences for four selected candidate genes. It is underpowered and cannot exclude effects, assign a causal gene or validate the genetic scores. A separate muscle series contains repeated samples and requires participant-aware analysis before use.

A [second public muscle-expression follow-up](stage6_gse117525_muscle_candidate_expression_review.md) found postselected SMAD5 and RBM6 group associations, but the source's `PROM` title label is perfectly aligned with frailty group and published analyses report batch differences. The earlier RNA-seq dataset did not detect these differences. These are confounded expression hypotheses, not replicated shared genetic mechanisms or score validation.

A further [public muscle sarcopenia expression screen](stage6_gse167186_sarcopenia_expression_review.md) found no four-gene BH-adjusted association in 24 sarcopenic and 29 healthy older participants. Sex was not available in the GEO sample metadata and the outcome is not FI/FFS, so it cannot resolve the earlier confounded group differences or validate the score.

The neuronal reporter [AI baseline uncertainty check](stage6_neuronal_mpra_ml_baseline_review.md) gives a conditional test-variant bootstrap AUROC range of 0.396–0.638 around 0.517. It does not account for regional dependence or demonstrate disease-score prediction.

A [nonagenarian PBMC expression follow-up](stage6_gse40366_frailty_expression_review.md) did not detect differences for four postselected genes in 29 frail versus 38 non-frail participants. This small blood analysis lacks individual genotype and sleep data and does not establish the absence of effects or validate scores; Vitality 90+ also contributes to the earlier longevity GWAS.

A [four-study expression comparison](stage6_candidate_expression_cross_study_review.md) places the candidate genes' muscle and blood observations together. The one study with two within-panel signals has substantial potential study-group confounding; other small, heterogeneous studies did not detect them. This is not genetic or score replication.
