# NHANES 2011–2012 sleep question and grip: separate-cycle follow-up

In the public 2011–2012 cycle, 1,461 examined adults aged 60 or older had a valid SLQ050 response and combined grip measurement. Using the same survey-weighted, age- and sex-adjusted regression as the prior 2013–2014 analysis, those who reported ever telling a health professional about trouble sleeping had an estimated **0.19 kg higher** combined grip strength (95% CI −1.43 to 1.80 kg; p=0.81). In the 2013–2014 cycle, the estimate was −0.56 kg (95% CI −2.22 to 1.09 kg). Both intervals include meaningful negative and positive differences; neither provides evidence of no association. The difference between these point estimates was not formally tested.

The [sleep question](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2011/DataFiles/SLQ_G.htm) concerns reporting trouble sleeping to a health professional at any time, and depends on reporting and access; it does not establish insomnia. The [grip outcome](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2011/DataFiles/MGX_G.htm) adds the best measurement of each hand and is a single physical function measure, not FI or Fried's five-component frailty score. The analysis is cross-sectional and does not establish causal or temporal direction. Age is top-coded at 80. Complete-case exclusion can bias estimates, and adjustment for age and sex does not remove all confounding.

We specified the same analysis before loading the follow-up outcome data in the [local protocol](stage6_nhanes_2011_replication_protocol.md). This is a separate-cycle follow-up, not a blinded or prospectively registered trial. Of 1,687 examined older adults, 226 had missing combined grip and none had invalid sleep responses. Of the 1,461 analyzed, 392 answered yes and 1,069 no. Survey design degrees of freedom were 17 and model residual degrees of freedom 14. Examination weights, the full examination-sample design, and a complete-case domain were used with R survey 4.5. Separate cycles have no shared public sequence IDs, but that check alone does not establish that no person could have been sampled twice or establish independence from every prior study. Published studies have used these cycles for related sleep–frailty questions; this is not a new genetic replication.
