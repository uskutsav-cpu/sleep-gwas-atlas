# FI and Fried phenotype: clinical mortality prediction in the same people

This note compares **measured clinical frailty instruments**, not genetic risk scores. The comparisons provide context for the project's FI–FFS genetic correlation; they do not measure genetic correlation or insomnia risk.

## Same-participant evidence

A [2022 systematic review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13643-022-02052-w) identified eight prospective studies of community-dwelling adults aged 65 or older that evaluated both FI and a Fried-type phenotype for all-cause mortality. Across studies, reported AUC ranges were 0.66–0.84 for continuous FI and 0.63–0.80 for continuous Fried-type measures; categorical FI ranged 0.60–0.80 and categorical Fried-type measures 0.57–0.79. Follow-up ranged from 2 to 7 years, and score construction and adjustment varied. The authors concluded that both approaches had modest, broadly comparable discrimination, with continuous scores generally more informative than categories. This is a synthesis of heterogeneous study ranges, not a pooled head-to-head effect or proof that the instruments are interchangeable.

A separate [2025 prospective study of older inpatients](https://eurjmedres.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s40001-025-02663-8) analyzed 917 patients with five-year mortality follow-up. In its Table 2, using the scores rather than binary categories, the reported mortality AUC was 0.742 (95% CI 0.699–0.786) for a comprehensive-geriatric-assessment FI and 0.726 (0.680–0.771) for Fried. The reported paired DeLong p for the FI-versus-Fried AUC comparison was 0.364. The point estimate favors FI in this setting, but the reported comparison does not establish a difference; it also does not establish equivalence. The abstract gives 0.724 for the CGA-FI AUC while Table 2 and the main text give 0.742; the table/main-text value and discrepancy are identified explicitly here. These inpatient instruments, patients and endpoint are not the project’s FI and FFS GWAS phenotypes.

## Implications for the project

1. **Do not turn genetic correlation into clinical prediction:** the project's FI–FFS genetic correlation around 0.75 describes shared genome-wide additive genetic components in specific GWAS datasets, not the AUC or similarity of clinical risk classification.
2. **Use the same outcome and sample for comparison:** a future score analysis should test FI and FFS genetic scores in the same independent participants with a prespecified outcome and time horizon. Use paired estimates of their performance difference, calibration and a clinical baseline model. The published FI R² and FFS AUC from different cohorts are not directly comparable.
3. **Preserve information where useful:** dichotomizing FI or FFS can change both overlap and prediction. The review’s observation favors investigating continuous scores, but it does not imply a continuous score is always superior in every setting or treatment decision.
4. **Keep sleep overlap explicit:** these clinical comparisons are not evidence that the genetic insomnia–frailty association persists after removing insomnia and fatigue from the source FI.
