# Provisional BBJ insomnia allele-count check

**This is a quality-control warning, not a replication result.** It was first identified in an interrupted stream. The exact rs12524473 row has since been recovered in the complete stream, which passed its gzip integrity check; see the [completed candidate review](stage6_bbj_insomnia_candidate_review.md). The original partial file remains incomplete and cannot establish variant absence.

One 22-marker candidate, rs12524473 at GRCh37 chr6:14,105,768, has exact A/G pair matching in the completed extract. Its source A1/A2 are A/G; reported G frequency is 0.000042086, with an imputed G dosage count of 15.0039 among N=178,253. The case G frequency is 0.00128535 and control G frequency 0.000039367. The case frequency corresponds approximately to one G dosage copy across 389 cases if the same denominators are used; frequencies and counts are derived from imputation, not direct verified hard-call counts. Source beta for G is 39.76 with SE 14.26, and its reported saddlepoint p is 0.00529 versus normal-approximation p 4.67×10⁻¹⁰. Such a large estimate with very few allele copies is unstable and cannot support a meaningful replicated association, score performance, or a causal interpretation. High reported imputation information (0.9907) does not resolve low allele count or small case count.

The [SAIGE documentation](https://github-wiki-see.page/m/weizhouUMICH/SAIGE/wiki/SAIGE-Hands-On-Practice-%2809-2021-updated%29) identifies beta as the Allele2 effect in its standard output; source-specific conventions should still be confirmed, and the source case/control frequencies should be checked against the full file. [SAIGE's paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6119127/) discusses saddlepoint calibration under case–control imbalance. The extremely large difference between the two reported p-values for this variant illustrates why assuming a normal-approximation p-value for all candidates is unsafe. It is not a correction for selection or a proof that the saddlepoint estimate is reliable for this locus.

The comparison-preparation script now records source case/control frequencies, allele dosage count, minor dosage count, both p-values, and a **descriptive** flag for minor dosage count below 20. That threshold is a review flag, not a validated exclusion rule; finalize and justify any exclusion or score test before interpreting the full dataset. For highly unbalanced binary outcomes, avoid a naive weighted-Z statistic based on beta/SE without evaluating its compatibility with the target model, rare allele behavior and score covariance. Do not rank results by nominal p-values from the interrupted stream.

The current 22-marker prototype counts **A**, not G. If the source's G-effect convention holds, its A-oriented beta would be approximately −39.76; this is an orientation exercise with an unstable estimate, not evidence of protection. The previous [reference-frequency table](22_score_reference_group_allele_frequencies.csv) used the earlier G orientation for this marker, so its EAS value 0.001984 is a G frequency and must not be read as the current score's A frequency. That 504-person 1000 Genomes EAS panel is a small multi-population reference, not the BBJ sample; the difference from the BBJ frequency does not by itself establish a coding error. The score's [orientation crosswalk](shared22_allele_orientation_crosswalk.csv) records the earlier and current directions. In BBJ, conditional on the source coding, nearly everyone would carry two copies of the counted A allele, leaving little variation for that marker to contribute to an individual score comparison.

## Study-wide precision context

The published browser counts (389 cases, 177,864 controls) correspond to a case fraction of 0.218% and a simple case-control effective sample size `4 / (1/389 + 1/177864) = 1,552.6`. This is a rough information-equivalent comparison to an equally balanced case-control design, not the actual sample count, an exact power calculation, or a substitute for the mixed model. It makes clear why the large number of controls cannot compensate for only 389 diagnosed cases, especially for the very rare candidate. Participant relatedness, imputation, phenotype ascertainment, regression adjustment and allele frequency further affect information. A non-significant result in this study cannot rule out a modest association.
