# Methods — proposed manuscript text

## Study design and participants

We conducted an exploratory analysis of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 public-use data. The National Center for Health Statistics used a stratified, multistage probability design to sample the civilian, noninstitutionalized US population. Participants were included if their age at screening was at least 60 years and their mobile examination center (MEC) examination weight was positive. The public age variable is topcoded at 80 years. Each cycle's demographic, sleep, depression, physical function, medical conditions, blood-pressure, diabetes, urology, health-care utilization, prescription medication, body-measurement, grip, activity, glycohemoglobin, and complete blood-count files were merged by cycle and participant sequence number; prescription-level records were reduced to participant-level prescription-count fields after checking within-person consistency. CDC approved NHANES data collection and obtained participant consent; this secondary analysis used publicly released, deidentified files.

The primary comparison additionally required the sleep exposure and each compared FI version to meet its completeness rule. We retained the full positive-MEC-weight survey sample when constructing the design, then defined older-adult and analytic domains to preserve variance estimation. We report unweighted participant counts and design-weighted estimates.

## Sleep measures

The primary exposure was SLQ050, whether the participant had ever told a doctor or other health professional about trouble sleeping (yes versus no). Refusals, don't-know responses and missing values were excluded. This question measures lifetime disclosure and may depend on access to care; it does not identify current insomnia. As a sensitivity analysis we used DPQ030, a two-week symptom item encompassing trouble falling or staying asleep **or sleeping too much**; contrasts were more than half the days/nearly every day versus less often, and any versus no symptoms. These sensitivity outcomes excluded DPQ030 and the fatigue item from the FI.

## Frailty measures and audit

We adapted a published 49-item FI applied to NHANES 2011–2014 (Liu et al., https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-025-05752-9), using the standard deficit-accumulation concept and 0–1 coding. The candidate items comprised one cognition, 15 function, seven mood, 13 comorbidity, five health/utilization, two anthropometric/performance and six laboratory items. The index equals the sum of observed coded deficits divided by the number observed, with a score calculated only if at least 80% of that version's constituent items were observed. The index has not been independently validated here and is not the Atkins UK Biobank FI. An independent rowwise implementation reread every original CDC source field and rederived each of the 49 items; the source-value, item-level and score-level comparisons are supplied in Supplementary Table `supplement_item_audit_checks.csv`.

The full 49-item FI includes DPQ030 (sleep disturbance or excessive sleep) and DPQ040 (tiredness/little energy). We constructed a 48-item version omitting DPQ030, a 48-item version omitting DPQ040, and a 47-item version omitting both. No other item directly asks about sleep, fatigue, tiredness or exhaustion; indirect relationships through medical diagnoses, medication use and function remain possible. All four scores were compared in the same participants. The primary endpoint was the continuous 47-item score. Alternative definitions excluded all seven mood items and all five care/general-health items, excluded laboratory items, excluded BMI, or excluded the walking and stair items in addition to sleep and fatigue. A 28-item sensitivity subset retained only items with ≤5% missing in each cycle; because it has fewer than 30 items and was selected from these data, it is not considered a validated FI. We report both the 80%-observed rule and an all-items-observed analysis.

The operational choices and differences from the published table are itemized in `supplement_49_item_audit.csv`. Key choices were KIQ005 for any urine leakage, a sex/BMI-specific low dominant-hand grip proxy, obesity at BMI ≥30 kg/m², and coding PFQ054-reported need for walking equipment as a proxy for the two skipped walking/steps items. The 2011–2012 health-care visit category 4–9 spans the published ≤5/>5 boundary and was left missing; the 2013–2014 categories and question wording differ. Such decisions, the source FI itself, and the analysis were not prospectively registered for this project.

For context we also reconstructed an adapted five-component Fried-like phenotype and an exhaustion-excluded four-component mean. We used self-reported loss >10 lb and the WHQ060 intention response, sex/BMI-specific grip thresholds, DPQ040 for exhaustion, quarter-mile walking difficulty for slowness, and a GPAQ-derived energy-expenditure proxy for activity. These are not identical to the CHS original and the adapted ≥3 threshold is not a diagnostic Fried score. A complete audit is provided in `frailty_construct_audit.md`. The previous four-component score was independently reconstructed and checked against the earlier dataset.

## Statistical analysis

For pooled analyses we used WTMEC2YR/2, the public masked strata and PSUs nested within cycle, and Taylor-linearized variance estimates in R `survey` 4.5. The full pooled design had 32 degrees of freedom, with 17 and 15 in the separate 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 designs. We fit survey-weighted linear regressions of each FI version on sleep disclosure, age, sex, and cycle. The same-participant change in associations after item removal was estimated by regressing the within-person full-minus-excluded FI difference on the same covariates. An additional model included categorical race/ethnicity, education, family income-to-poverty ratio, and ever smoking; we compared it with a minimally adjusted model in the identical complete-covariate sample. Race/ethnicity is a social and demographic variable, not a genetic ancestry measure. Adjusted odds and prevalence ratios for the exploratory FI >0.21 cutoff were estimated with quasibinomial and log-link quasipoisson survey models, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses considered separate cycles, a cycle-by-exposure interaction, sex stratification and interaction, age ≥65, complete item data, alternative FI compositions, the recent symptom exposure, and comparisons with the previous physical scores. For missingness, we described each item by cycle and sleep exposure and modeled FI nonavailability using sleep disclosure and observed covariates. As a response-weighting sensitivity, we estimated the probability of meeting the common-score rule using sleep disclosure, age, sex, cycle, race/ethnicity, education, income and smoking, then applied inverse estimated response probabilities to the fully adjusted model. Both the propensity and outcome models were refitted in each stratified delete-one-PSU jackknife replicate. The analysis assumes the included covariates adequately explain selection, and cannot address missing-not-at-random mechanisms. Predicted response-probability and weight diagnostics are supplied.

Two-sided 95% confidence intervals and p-values use the design degrees of freedom. Holm adjustment is applied to the seven primary FI/paired contrasts and separately to 33 exploratory regression tests. These analysis families were defined retrospectively for this exercise and do not convert the study into confirmatory evidence. The earlier custom Python Taylor results were independently reproduced using R `survey`, and the primary standard error was checked by delete-one-PSU jackknife.

## Mortality construct check

We linked the publicly available CDC 2019 linked mortality files by cycle and SEQN. In older MEC participants eligible for linkage with an observed sleep/fatigue-excluded FI, survey-weighted logistic and quasipoisson models related vital status through 31 December 2019 to FI per 0.1 unit, adjusting for age, sex and cycle. Of 3,389 participants, 765 were recorded deceased. CDC states that public-use vital status is not perturbed, but some follow-up times and causes of death are replaced with synthetic values; we used only linkage eligibility and vital status and did not analyze altered follow-up times or cause of death. Follow-up duration varies between and within cycles, so the result is a construct/prognostic check, not a fixed-horizon risk or time-to-event analysis. It uses the same NHANES cohorts as the reference publication and is not external validation.

## Genetic-score provenance

The existing 15- and 22-marker postselected prototypes were recovered from the prior local project and compared with the actual project FI, insomnia and FFS GWAS summary files. We matched rsID, reported chromosome and position, allele pair, and signed coefficient after same/swapped orientation to the counted allele. All checks are recorded per variant and trait. We separately queried Ensembl's GRCh37 variation endpoint for reference-locus and allele inventory. Reference or summary-file agreement does not establish original source-build certainty, a biallelic source/target contrast, or target genotype coverage. A1 effect-allele interpretation in the FFS data remains an inference. No participant genotype or genetic-outcome analysis was performed.
