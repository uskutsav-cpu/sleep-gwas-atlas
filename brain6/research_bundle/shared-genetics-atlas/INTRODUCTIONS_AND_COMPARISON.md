## Brain6: sleep, ADHD and functional interpretation

Sleep disturbance and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often occur together, but the biological processes underlying their relationship remain uncertain. Insomnia is highly polygenic: large genome-wide association studies (GWAS), including [Jansen et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-018-0333-3) and [Watanabe et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-022-01124-w), have identified many associated regions and provide a foundation for testing overlap with other traits. An [ADHD GWAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10914347/) provides complementary evidence on ADHD genetics. Genome-wide genetic correlation summarizes the alignment of common-variant effects across the genome; it does not show that one condition causes the other or that a particular variant affects both.

Two 2026 studies have already examined this overlap. [Zu et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13493937/) combined longitudinal sleep and ADHD-symptom analyses in adolescents with cross-trait genetics; they reported insomnia–ADHD loci including rs62264764 in the LSAMP/LINC03051 region and a chromosome 5 signal. [Xue et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41065713/) examined insomnia alongside 12 psychiatric disorders using complementary cross-trait methods and reported shared loci and neuronal cell-type and pathway enrichments. Their approaches and phenotypes differ: longitudinal symptom relationships, aggregate genetic correlation, locus association and cell-type enrichment answer different questions. Neither enrichment nor positional gene mapping alone identifies the gene or cell type through which a particular variant acts.

[AlphaGenome](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-10014-0) adds a sequence-based approach for predicting molecular readouts and comparing reference and alternative alleles. Such predictions can guide experiments, but they are not measured allele effects or probabilities that a person will develop a disease. Brain6's pilot applies selected AlphaGenome annotations to previously implicated insomnia–ADHD variants and compares them with fine-mapping and measured molecular associations. For rs62264764, a large predicted frontal-cortex DNase change motivates investigation of nearby genes, while [brain-cell QTL and colocalization follow-up](variant_context/rs62264764_brain_xqtl/RESULTS.md) leaves the disease-relevant gene and signal unresolved. A strong chromosome 5 trait–trait colocalization is encouraging but model-dependent; the failed local-correlation quality gates remain failed.

A [2026 adult ADHD-symptom preprint](replication/adult_adhd_symptoms_2026/REVIEW.md) also reports domain-specific genetic patterns, including a FOXP2-annotated signal and an insomnia correlation; it is preliminary and does not identify the same Brain6 causal variant.

The prospective contribution is **functional prioritization and a transparent, multiscale comparison**, not the first discovery of insomnia–ADHD overlap or of rs62264764. A rigorous next test is whether permitted AlphaGenome-derived annotations improve identification of independently measured functional effects beyond simple association and fine-mapping baselines, evaluated on held-out loci and studies. Shared source cohorts, LD, reference ancestry, tissue and model uncertainty must be documented before claiming replication or a causal mechanism.

## Sleep and frailty: comparing related but distinct phenotypes

Frailty describes vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, but common measures capture different aspects of that vulnerability. The frailty index (FI) counts accumulated health deficits and is commonly expressed as a proportion of those assessed; the [Atkins et al. GWAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8441299/) uses a broad deficit-based measure. The Fried frailty score (FFS) combines weakness, exhaustion, weight loss, low activity and slow walking; [Ye et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10651618/) analyzed this five-component phenotype in UK Biobank. The [hospital frailty risk score (HFRS)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-025-00925-y) instead uses weighted hospital diagnosis codes. Their different components, ascertainment, populations and statistical models mean that FI, FFS and HFRS should not be treated as interchangeable measurements.

Sleep matters for this comparison both as a potential contributor to health and because of measurement overlap. The FI used in the project includes insomnia and tiredness, while FFS includes exhaustion. An association between sleep and these scores could partly reflect shared questionnaire content as well as common biology, behavior, environment or illness. HFRS offers a distinct hospital-based comparison, but differences in clinical ascertainment introduce other sources of variation. Phenotypic correlation between scores measured in the same people is a different quantity from genetic correlation estimated from GWAS; neither alone identifies a causal pathway.

Prior work already demonstrates important overlap. [Song et al.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnagi.2024.1358996/full) investigated genome-wide and regional insomnia–FI genetics, and [Che et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11812435/) examined sleep traits against both FI and FFS using Mendelian randomization. [Foote et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-025-02269-0) used genomic structural equation modeling across frailty deficits, including insomnia and tiredness. Shared sleep–frailty genetics, multivariate frailty analysis and FI–FFS comparison are established prior work. The present atlas can add a consistent comparison across sleep measures and FI, FFS and HFRS, with matched marker support, explicit uncertainty, transparent local-QC outcomes and independent-source follow-up.

The key unresolved genetic question is whether sleep–frailty relationships persist when the relevant sleep and exhaustion items are removed in the **same participants and model**. A [published category-excluded FI](component_exclusion/mak_category_fi/RESULTS.md) removes insomnia and fatigue together with other items, but its full SNP-level statistics were not found in the checked release. Phenotypic precedents include [Zhu et al.](component_exclusion/zhu_observational/REVIEW.md), who removed an insomnia item, and the [2026 U.S. POINTER trial analysis](component_exclusion/us_pointer_2026/REVIEW.md), which repeated an intervention comparison without four sleep components. These answer different questions and do not supply a matched sleep-excluded FI GWAS. Walking pace and grip provide useful physical-function context, yet neither is a matched item-excluded frailty composite. Separating these influences requires appropriately constructed or validated summary data and does not by itself prove causality.

## Hypotheses for future evaluation

These are questions, not established results. Freeze phenotypes, cohorts, primary tests and multiplicity families before confirmatory analyses.

1. **Sleep–ADHD architecture:** insomnia and ADHD show positive genome-wide genetic correlation, while individual shared-signal evidence differs across loci. Test generalization using independent cohorts and suitable LD.
2. **Functional priority:** permitted AlphaGenome annotations improve held-out experimental variant-effect retrieval beyond fine-mapping and association baselines. Use LD-block and study holdouts, calibration and matched controls; follow output terms.
3. **Frailty definition:** sleep genetic correlations differ across FI, FFS and HFRS. Use matched marker support and covariance-aware comparisons, while recognizing source and construct differences remain confounded.
4. **Shared measurement:** an FI excluding sleep and fatigue, and an FFS excluding exhaustion, retain some sleep association. Compare matched participants and models; a reduction cannot automatically be assigned to one item or pathway.
5. **Molecular mechanism:** credible variants at the chromosome 3 or 5 loci alter specific transcripts in relevant cells. Test with appropriately controlled multi-signal colocalization and independent allele-specific perturbation; proximity and predictions alone are insufficient.
